Communication/Games

When someone practices a field of expertise long enough, they will naturally start to pick up on the fact that they are "speaking" some kind of "language" of that field. I'm not referring to jargon, though that is related, I'm talking about the wordless signals that one expert can convey to or pick up from another without friction. When a woodworker sees a piece of furniture, they can often deconstruct the process of creating it in their mind and start to intuit and understand choices made with respect to that construction. Anyone who plays any kind of competitive game has felt this as an argument; when you play basketball, while dribbling on the approach to the opposing team's basket and a player is standing in the way, how do you make the decision on which direction to go or how to change your plan? Both you and your immediate opponent are aware that you have a set of possible options to take. Both of you are "having an argument" in that either they will prove their side correct by taking the ball or forcing you to make a pass, or you will prove your side correct by finding a way around them or making a successful shot. None of this needs words, but a transfer of understanding is still occurring.

The process of communication through spoken language occurs in 5 parts:

  1. Have a thought, translate it into words inside your mind as best you can.
  2. Produce those words with your mouth, turning them into sound.
  3. The sound you produced travels as wavelike vibrations through the medium of air.
  4. Someone's ears receive the vibrations and they receive the spoken words as best as the medium allowed.
  5. That person unpacks the language and turns them back into thoughts as best they can.

Notice that at each step of this process, there exist myriad possibilities for mistakes. A thought can be poorly translated, one can misspeak/flub pronunciation, the air can be full of other noises, someone may not hear it correctly, or even assuming all of that went a-ok maybe it just hits their mind wrong and they don't unpack the intended meaning as you meant it to be unpacked. Compare this to the basketball example where the interacting parts are the actions rather than the words and consider the nature of the exchange. In spoken communication, all 5 steps listed have to occur back and forth, one after another; in the basketball example, think about the simultaneous exchange occurring where you are processing your set of possibilities while the opponent is doing the same. The competitive example involves multiple simultaneous exchanges in the same amount of time a speaker can take a single "turn". This does not mean that the non-spoken example is any kind of strict improvement, but does suggest that there are different vectors of expression in each example as well as different interactions around timing.

However, I think the biggest difference between these two different forms of communication involve the medium through which the message travels as it leaves the consciousness of one and before it enters the awareness of another. The interface between conversationalists is sound, the state of the conversation, as well as the state of mind and theory of mind of each conversationalists, but sight can also play into things relative to body language, expressions, etc. The interface between the basketball players includes combinations of sight, each player's proprioception, each player's state of mind and theory of mind, and the current state of the game itself. A misstep in any step of the communication process between conversationalists results in a fuzzy misunderstanding that could lead to confusion, argument, or time spent explaining; a misstep in any step of the process between the basketball players results in a necessary change in the game state due to the time pressure of the game as well as a new resulting state with respect the physics of the players' bodies.

I'm sure I'm not alone in often struggling as a conversationalist when not allotted the benefit of time to prepare responses; there are times I feel more able to "converse" in the language of a game or system better than I could in English. I often feel as though I understand others better when watching them play games than I do when talking to them. Very often when I meet a new person, if it's not in a professional context, I will ask to play some kind of game with them or ask what games they normally play and try to just listen to see if I can understand what draws them to whatever games they mention. This is where I begin to fail with my words: I can't describe exactly how I feel when I watch people play games and what I am able to gather from it, but I'm often able to understand at least a portion of whether I can trust the person, how much that trust should be guarded, how likely the person is use to lateral thinking in problem solving, something of their style of problem solving, and their ability to work with others. None of these are exact or necessarily correct as I gather them, but as an autistic person it gives me a much better starting position to work with than body language, facial expression, or small talk would. To put it another way, I often feel far less friction "talking" to others when I do so through the language of a game than when I do so with spoken language.

Friction in communication can feel like self-consciousness, social anxiety, misunderstandings, stumbling on your words or using the wrong one by accident... a whole host of potential mistakes. As a teenager, I didn't really understand how people could talk to one another so smoothly; it wasn't until I was in my early 20's that I started thinking about conversations like a game of Magic: The Gathering where each "player" had hidden "cards" and any time someone "played" a "card" there was a reason they were doing so in the first place. Whether the person decided to talk about a given topic due to boredom or due to some agenda is still somewhat indescipherable, but when I started looking at the known information between people as the "playing field" and new information as a "newly played card", I could start conceptualizing my own thoughts in the same way. Now I operate on a kind of tit-for-tat model where in a friendly, non-professional context, I hold no secrets or hidden cards unless they aren't my secrets to share, if it's clear that they are holding hidden cards then I back off from open book, and if it's clear a lie is being told I'm going to scrutinize my "plays" as much as I would if money was on the line in a tournament. Being able to model my conversation choices as game moves unlocked quite a bit for me, and at this point I don't really have to actively think about it as much. This gets so much easier if I'm able to simply play a few rounds of Guilty Gear or Smash Bros with someone; no words have to be exchanged but suddenly I feel much more confident about my level of engagement and ability to speak with them afterward.

Personal Dilemmas/Pitfalls

My husband can attest that when I start getting stressed, my language starts getting more... specific. As soon as I realize I've been misunderstood, I start to second guess my words to the point that I over-engineer the first step of verbal communication. I can feel the shape of the thought I need to communicate, and I can feel the distance between the thought and my vocabulary; the unfortunate part is that too often a perfect translation of the thought into words simply does not exist, so I have to limit closer and closer to a "good enough" answer. I begin to trip over myself, repeat what I've said in different ways, fall silent for a bit while I consider options, or sometimes I'll give up on talking entirely and just start drawing pictures. "Learn the rules so that you can break them" and "Chesterton's Fence" are two ideas often working towards communicating the same concept, both have different ways of being understood and misunderstood as well as contexts where they individually work best; where I struggle is choosing the correct one to optimize closeness to my original meaning and being understood.

In Bloodborne, the dealings and actions of outer gods that take place in higher realms of existence have consequences and meaning in the physical realm the player resides in. The words of the outer beings are captured in the form of pictographic runes that bestow power, made by a madman named Caryll "who was able to hear these utterings and express them into these strange and bizarre markings". Magic: The Gathering has creatures called "Eldrazi" that live in the space between planes of existence called "The Blind Eternities"; an elder dragon named Ugin described their physical manifestations in a way I think about often: "Imagine that you reach your hand into a pond. The fish below the surface sees a five-headed monster, and cannot perceive the man attached to it. It mistakes a hangnail for an eye because the truth is beyond its imagining." These are the closest metaphors I can find to explain my anxiety around spoken communication. In a majority of cases, no matter the words I choose in the moment the palm connecting the five-headed monster cannot push through.

The biggest pitfall I run into is when I need to communicate two ideas simultaneously; there are many times when the order in which ideas are presented suggests further intent or meaning, but I need to communicate both. If I'm trying to say "I think I am wrong here AND I think you are wrong, but we can work together to solve this", this message contains three parts. The best case clearly begins with cooperation upfront where the opening move is "I want to work with you to solve this." After that, however, if I say "you are wrong" to start with, my own admission of being wrong is softened and it may appear that I'm trying to minimize my portion; if I say "I am wrong", the opposite occurs. Both ideas could be as important as each other, and simply saying "I think we are both wrong" can come off as dismissive; there needs to be the full sequence of steps of admission -> explanation -> solution in order to move on for each portion. The linear nature of spoken language and time itself contributes to the imperfection of our communication, time is cruel in even the subtlest of ways.

Machines/Interfacing

Computers do not have to deal with these ambiguities. Relative to natural language, there exists no grey area inside a CPU. There are 0's, there are 1's, and there is the order in which those 0's and 1's arrive. The assembly code that gets translated to 0's and 1's also has no ambiguity in how it is transformed, the most confusing part of that has more to do with what kind of processor you have than anything else. The programming languages you code in are designed to have no ambiguity as they break down line by line, but understanding that process in its entirety is opaque as hell. It can very well be done, but it takes a lot of work to get to the point where the lack of ambiguity becomes clear. Every programming language you've come into contact with works by breaking down each line or block into individual expressions, breaks down those expressions into individual operations, then translates those individual operations into assembly code. This is ultimately a bit of a reductive explanation due to how much work goes into actually accomplishing this task, but that's the basic structure. The base level is always in 0's and 1's, assembly code reads each command one-by-one (like an assembly line!) and then "assembles" into those 0's and 1's, and programming languages are automatically read and "compiled" into assembly code. Notice how that we've created stack after stack after stack of automated tools here.

Programming langauges are tools that help us as humans more easily write large amounts of code so that we don't have to fiddle with each and every individual operation; effectively, programming languages are automation tools for generating assembly code and assembly code is an automation/simplifying tool for writing machine-readable code. Since the vacuum tube we've been building layer after layer of tools to make larger and larger projects possible without herculean effort. Famously, Rollercoaster Tycoon (1999) was programmed by Chris Sawyer almost entirely in assembly; this is one of the few tasks I know of that is most like creating the universe in order to bake an apple pie from scratch. All compilers will produce some amount of extra or inefficient assembly code relative to programming the assembly by hand, but today's compilers have minimized the hell out of that overhead; at the time however, Sawyer's 13 labors afford the game quite a bit of optimization relative to the alternatives. Using modern tools, however, the gains that Sawyer earned are not nearly as worth it; year after year the computer scientists working away at different compilers have drawn that gap closer and closer to being closed. Though it will likely never be fully possible to eliminate that overhead, we're now talking about saving fractions of fractions of microseconds.

Simultaneously, there's a sort of "purity" to programming in assembly in the way that some mathematics can be called "pure"; that's not a judgement that x86 is better than C or whatever but it is to say that there exists very little space between your language used and the effect produced. The only space between your language and the effect at that point is the physical reality of electricity flowing across metal and silicon. With every layer added between the language used and the effect produced, another layer of runes must be created and another layer of pond exists for the hand to enter. The CPU won't struggle any more or less with any of these methods, it's still just reading 0's and 1's as it always does, but with every additional layer we as programmers have to do more and more to understand what went wrong when an error occurs. The hardest part of teaching students how to program in x86 is getting them to understand each and every memory movement taking place inside the registers of the CPU. Understanding the registers isn't difficult, those are just boxes that hold some number of bits; understanding individual instructions isn't difficult, each performs exactly one thing at a time and are all very explicit as to how they do so; even condition flags aren't bad, they're just a flow chart of what memory address to jump to given the result of the previous arithmetic instruction (that takes the longest out of the pieces listed but it's not to bad I promise). But getting a student to understand that they need to use movq to copy a value in a register, subtract from that register at the end of their loop, then use the correct conditional jump to go to the intended position all together is difficult because the student already has it in their head that all they have to do is "if [condition] do x, else do y" and have not had to consider what the computer is actually doing internally to make that work.
